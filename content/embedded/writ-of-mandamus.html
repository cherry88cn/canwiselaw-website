Federal Court Remedy

Writ of Mandamus

A mandamus application asks the Federal Court to require a public authority to perform a legal duty—for example, to make a decision where an immigration matter has remained unreasonably delayed.

What It Means

“We Command”

Mandamus is a discretionary court order directing a public official or authority to carry out a duty the law requires. It does not direct the decision-maker to approve an application; rather, in an appropriate case, it may compel the authority to act and make a decision.

The remedy is exceptional and fact-specific. Delay alone is not automatically enough, and there is no universal number of months after which mandamus becomes available.

Key Considerations

When Mandamus May Be Considered

Public Legal Duty

The authority must owe a clear public duty to act, and the applicant must have satisfied the conditions giving rise to that duty.

Unreasonable Delay

The Court considers the nature and complexity of the process, whether the applicant caused delay, and whether the authority has provided a satisfactory, case-specific justification.

Prior Demand

Applicants generally should first ask the authority to perform the duty or decide the matter and allow a reasonable opportunity to respond.

Discretionary Relief

The Court also considers adequate alternatives, practical value, equitable concerns and the balance of convenience before deciding whether an order should issue.

Illustrative Federal Court Decisions

How the Courts Assess Delay

2026

Alnuaimi v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 647

The Court granted mandamus in an overseas spousal sponsorship matter after a delay approaching seven years. The decision applied the Apotex requirements and the Conille delay factors, and found that generalized reliance on prolonged security screening did not adequately explain the particular delay on the evidence before the Court.

2026

Benison v. Canada (Royal Canadian Mounted Police External Review Committee), 2026 FCA 53

The Federal Court of Appeal reaffirmed the Apotex framework and clarified that significant prejudice is not an independent prerequisite to establishing unreasonable delay for mandamus. Open-ended resource and backlog explanations must be connected to the actual files, and concerns about “queue jumping” are not an automatic bar. The Court ordered the tribunal to complete the outstanding reviews within six months.

Read the decision
2023

Chen v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2023 FC 885

In a study-permit delay case, the Court assessed the complete procedural record and whether the requirements for discretionary relief were established. The decision illustrates that elapsed time must be evaluated together with the nature of the process, the explanations for delay and the other mandamus criteria.

Read the decision
2011

Abdalla v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2011 FC 988

The decision illustrates that the availability of another practical route—including temporary status in the circumstances of that case—may weigh against mandamus. The adequacy of any alternative depends on the particular record.

Read the decision
1998

Conille v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 1998 CanLII 9097 (FC)

Conille provides the familiar contextual inquiry for unreasonable delay: whether the delay is longer than the nature of the process requires, whether the applicant is responsible, and whether the authority has given a satisfactory justification.

Read the decision
1993

Apotex Inc. v. Canada (Attorney General), 1993 CanLII 3004 (FCA)

Apotex remains the leading framework for mandamus, addressing the public duty, the applicant's clear right to performance, any discretionary element, adequate alternatives, practical value, equitable bars and the balance of convenience.

Important: These decisions illustrate legal principles; they do not create a fixed waiting period or guarantee the same result in another matter. Each case turns on its evidence, procedural history and current law.

Frequently Asked Questions

Mandamus and Immigration Delay

How long should I wait before considering mandamus?

There is no fixed waiting period. Mandamus is an exceptional remedy and is generally considered a remedy of last resort. It is usually more appropriate to consider mandamus where processing has materially and significantly exceeded IRCC’s published processing time, rather than where the delay is only brief.

Published processing times are an important benchmark, but they are not legal deadlines, and exceeding them does not automatically make a delay unreasonable. The assessment will depend on the complete history of the application, including the type and complexity of the matter, the length and causes of the delay, whether any part of the delay is attributable to the applicant, what steps IRCC or another authority has taken, and whether any explanation for the delay is specific and satisfactory.

Should I contact IRCC before filing?

Yes. Before seeking mandamus, an applicant should normally have asked IRCC to act and given it a reasonable opportunity to respond. This is important because the legal test for mandamus includes a prior demand for performance of the duty and a reasonable time for the decision-maker to comply.

IRCC webforms, status enquiries, requests for GCMS notes or enquiries through a Member of Parliament may help clarify the status of the application and document previous efforts to obtain a decision. However, there is no fixed number or sequence of enquiries that must be completed in every case.

Where mandamus is being seriously considered, counsel will often send a formal demand letter requesting that IRCC make a decision within a reasonable period before commencing Federal Court proceedings. This gives IRCC a final opportunity to address the delay and helps establish that court intervention is being sought as a remedy of last resort.

What if security or background screening is pending?

Security screening may be relevant to the explanation for delay, but its existence does not by itself determine whether the delay is reasonable. The Court examines the evidence and whether the explanation is connected to the actual file and the length of the delay.

Where there are specific and ongoing security concerns, a longer processing period may be justified. On the other hand, prolonged delay accompanied only by general statements that “security screening is in progress” may still support an application for mandamus, particularly where there is little evidence explaining why the screening has taken so long.

Will filing mandamus harm my application?

Using a lawful court process does not change the legal criteria governing the application. Mandamus does not remove the applicant's ongoing duty to provide truthful information, comply with requests and keep contact details current.

Mandamus addresses delay, not the merits of the application. IRCC remains responsible for assessing the application under the applicable legal requirements and may continue any outstanding processing required by law.

Federal Court proceedings should, however, be pursued responsibly. The Court has cautioned against repetitive or duplicative mandamus proceedings concerning the same outstanding matter, particularly where an earlier proceeding was not completed or was refused and no reasonable explanation is provided for filing again. In such circumstances, repeated proceedings may be considered an abuse of process and may result in cost consequences.

How long does the Federal Court process take?

Many mandamus applications are resolved before a Federal Court hearing is required. If the case continues through the leave stage, the Federal Court process follows a relatively structured timetable.

Generally, a mandamus application that proceeds all the way to a hearing will commonly take approximately 8–20 months from filing to hearing or disposition. Many cases, however, do not reach that stage because IRCC takes action on the underlying application while the Federal Court proceeding is ongoing.

What happens if leave is not granted?

If the Federal Court refuses leave, the mandamus proceeding ends and there will be no oral hearing. A refusal of leave cannot be appealed. The refusal does not, however, decide the merits of the underlying immigration application, which remains with IRCC for processing and determination.

A refusal of leave also does not necessarily mean that mandamus could never become appropriate in the future. Immigration delay is an evolving situation. If substantially more time passes, the circumstances of the application change, or new information becomes available concerning the cause or history of the delay, the situation may be reassessed.

A subsequent mandamus application should not simply repeat an earlier unsuccessful proceeding. The Federal Court has cautioned that filing a further mandamus application following an earlier refusal, without a reasonable explanation for doing so, may constitute an abuse of process and may result in cost consequences.
