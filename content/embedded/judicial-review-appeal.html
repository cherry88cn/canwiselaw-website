Judicial Review and Appeal

If an immigration application has been refused, CanWise Law can assess the reasons, identify available remedies, and provide representation before the Federal Court or the Immigration Appeal Division where applicable.

After a Negative Decision

Reviewing the Decision and Available Remedy

We analyze the decision, record and procedural history to identify arguable legal errors, unreasonable findings, factual misunderstandings or breaches of procedural fairness. The appropriate route depends on the type of decision, the governing legislation and whether a statutory right of appeal exists.

Where a remedy is available, the objective is ordinarily to seek a fair reconsideration or a new determination—not to guarantee a particular outcome.

Immigration Appeal Division

Appeals to the IAD

Sponsorship Appeals

Under s. 63(1) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, a Canadian citizen or permanent resident sponsor may appeal certain family-class refusals. The right belongs to the sponsor, and statutory bars can apply, including specified inadmissibility findings.

Removal Order Appeals

Permanent residents, protected persons and certain foreign nationals holding permanent resident visas may have a right of appeal under s. 63(2) or (3). Eligibility and statutory appeal bars require case-specific review.

Residency Obligation Appeals

Under s. 63(4), a permanent resident may appeal certain overseas determinations that the residency obligation in s. 28 was not met. Legal, factual and humanitarian considerations may be relevant.

Important: Appeal rights are technical and time limits may be short. Inadmissibility based on security, human or international rights violations, sanctions, serious criminality, organized criminality or certain misrepresentation findings may restrict an appeal.
Federal Court

Judicial Review

Where there is no statutory appeal—or where judicial review is otherwise the proper remedy—a party may ask the Federal Court to review an immigration or visa decision. Counsel examines whether the decision was unreasonable or legally flawed and prepares written submissions supported by the record, legislation and relevant jurisprudence.

Judicial review generally begins with an application for leave. If leave is granted, the Court schedules a hearing. The usual remedy, if the application succeeds, is to set aside the decision and return the matter for redetermination by a different decision-maker; the Court does not ordinarily substitute its own immigration decision.

Procedural fairness, the interpretation of legislation, material factual errors and constitutional issues may arise depending on the record.
